You check the account and your stomach drops. The stock collapsed, the startup folded, the "sure thing" turned out to be anything but. Money you worked hard for — maybe money meant for a home, retirement, or your children — has shrunk or vanished. You replay the decision over and over: the warning signs you ignored, the moment you could have sold. Shame mixes with fear about the future, and you wonder how you'll explain it, even to yourself.

## The Mindful Approach

The Buddha taught impermanence — that all conditioned things rise and pass away, including markets, fortunes, and certainties. Financial loss is a sharp encounter with this truth. It hurts, and that pain deserves acknowledgment. But much of your suffering comes from the second arrow: the self-blame, the endless replaying, the catastrophizing. You can feel the first arrow fully while declining to keep shooting yourself with the second.

- **Grieve the loss honestly.** Don't rush to minimize it or pretend you're fine. Money often represents security, dreams, and effort, and losing it is a real loss. Allow yourself to feel disappointment and fear. Emotions that are acknowledged tend to move through; those that are suppressed tend to linger and harden.
- **Replace self-punishment with learning.** You made a decision with the information and understanding you had at the time. Hindsight makes everything look obvious. Instead of berating yourself, ask calmly: "What can I learn about risk, research, or my own impulses?" Turn the loss into tuition rather than a life sentence.
- **Remember what the market can't take.** Your skills, relationships, health, and ability to earn and rebuild are still here. Net worth is not self-worth. Grounding yourself in what remains — and in values that no downturn can touch — restores a sense of stability when the numbers feel unstable.

## A Practice for Today

Sit down with a notebook and write two short lists. First, "What I lost" — be honest, and let yourself feel it. Second, "What I still have" — your abilities, your people, your health, the lessons this experience taught you. Read the second list slowly, twice. Then write one sentence describing how you'll approach financial decisions differently from now on. The loss is real, but it isn't the end of your story. You can rise from here, steadier and wiser.
