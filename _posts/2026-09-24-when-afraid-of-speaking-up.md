The meeting moves on, and the thought you had stays lodged in your throat. You rehearsed it, felt your pulse quicken, waited for the right pause — and the pause never came, or it came and you let it pass. Later, someone else says roughly what you were thinking and gets nodded at. You leave with a familiar ache: the sense that you shrank yourself again, that your silence spoke louder than anything you might have said.

## The Mindful Approach

Fear of speaking up usually protects something tender — a wish to belong, to avoid looking foolish, to stay safe. That fear isn't a flaw to crush; it's a nervous system doing its old job. But safety bought with constant silence has a cost. Right speech, in the Buddhist sense, is not only avoiding harmful words; it is also offering true and useful ones at the proper time. Your perspective is part of the whole, and withholding it leaves the whole incomplete.

- **Befriend the fear before you speak.** Notice the tight chest, the dry mouth, the racing thoughts, and name them gently: "This is fear arising." Naming creates a sliver of space between the feeling and your choice. You don't have to wait for the fear to vanish — you only have to stop letting it hold the microphone.
- **Start small and let courage accumulate.** You don't need to challenge the CEO tomorrow. Ask one clarifying question, add one sentence to a discussion, share one observation in a smaller group. Each small act of voice teaches your body that speaking is survivable. Confidence is less a trait you have than a track record you build.
- **Speak to contribute, not to perform.** Much of the dread comes from imagining an audience grading you. Shift the focus from how you'll be judged to what might help: a risk nobody named, an idea that could serve the team. When your intention is to offer rather than impress, the stakes feel lighter and your words land more cleanly.

## A Practice for Today

Before your next conversation or meeting, choose one thing you genuinely want to say — a question, a concern, a small idea. Write it down in a single sentence. When the moment comes, take one slow breath, feel your feet on the floor, and say it without over-explaining or apologizing. Afterward, notice that you're still standing, whatever the response. Each honest sentence you release makes the next one easier. Your voice belongs in the room.
