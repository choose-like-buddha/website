Every email gets cc'd, every decision second-guessed, every task checked before it's finished. Your manager asks for updates on updates and rewrites sentences you were proud of. You feel like a child being supervised, not a professional being trusted. Your motivation drains, your creativity shrinks, and you start double-checking everything out of anxious anticipation. Some days you wonder why they hired you at all if they won't let you actually do the job.

## The Mindful Approach

Micromanagement almost always reveals more about the manager than about you. It often stems from their own anxiety, pressure from above, or a need for control in an uncertain situation. Recognizing this can dissolve some of the sting. You can't control their behavior, but you can influence the relationship — and you can choose not to internalize their fear as a judgment on your ability. Equanimity here means staying grounded while gently working to build trust.

- **Don't take the hovering personally.** When someone checks every detail, it's easy to hear a verdict on your competence. Remind yourself that their need for control is their burden. Holding this view keeps you from sliding into defensiveness or self-doubt, and lets you respond from confidence rather than from wounded pride.
- **Anticipate their need for information.** Micromanagers usually calm down when they feel informed. Offer proactive updates before they ask — a brief daily summary, a shared progress tracker, early flags on risks. Paradoxically, giving a bit more visibility often earns you more freedom. Trust tends to grow when uncertainty shrinks.
- **Have an honest, respectful conversation.** If the pattern persists, request a calm discussion about working styles. Ask what reassurance they need and share what helps you do your best work — perhaps agreed check-in points and more autonomy between them. Framing it as a shared goal rather than a complaint opens the door to real change.

## A Practice for Today

Before your next interaction with your manager, take a slow breath and silently note: "Their anxiety is not my identity." Then send one proactive update they haven't asked for — clear, concise, confident. Watch how they respond. If the timing feels right this week, suggest a short conversation about how you can best keep them informed while working more independently. Meeting control with calm competence changes the dynamic slowly. Your steadiness can become their reassurance.
