You glance at the clock and your stomach drops. The minutes are slipping away faster than the traffic moves, and every red light feels like a personal insult. You're rehearsing apologies, calculating arrival times, gripping the wheel or tapping your foot on the train platform. Your body is racing even though you're standing still. By the time you arrive, you're flushed and scattered, and the lateness has already swallowed the part of your day that came before it.

## The Mindful Approach

Being late creates a gap between where you are and where you think you should be — and the mind rushes to fill that gap with worry. But anxiety has never shortened a commute. The moment you're in, stuck at this crossing or waiting on this platform, is still a real moment of your life. You can take responsibility for the delay while refusing to let it steal your composure. Calm is not indifference; it's simply the wiser way to arrive.

- **Separate what you can do from what you can't.** Send a brief, honest message letting people know. Choose the fastest reasonable route. After that, the outcome is in the hands of traffic lights and train schedules. Once you've done your part, continued fretting adds nothing but tension to your shoulders.
- **Notice the story you're telling.** Lateness often triggers a cascade: they'll be annoyed, I'm always like this, everything is ruined. Most of these thoughts are projections, not facts. A few minutes behind is usually forgiven far more easily than you imagine. Let the catastrophe shrink back to its true size.
- **Arrive fully, not frantically.** Before you walk in, pause for a single breath. Enter composed, apologize sincerely once, and then be present. People remember how you showed up far longer than when. A grounded late arrival beats a frazzled one every time.

## A Practice for Today

The next time you realize you're running behind, try this: send one clear message, then place a hand on your belly and take three slow breaths, feeling each one fill and empty you. Look around and name three things you can see right now — a tree, a stranger's coat, the color of the sky. This anchors you in the present instead of the imagined scene ahead. Later, reflect gently on what caused the delay, without blame. The minutes are gone, but your peace doesn't have to go with them.
