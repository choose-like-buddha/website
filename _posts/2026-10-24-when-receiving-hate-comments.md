You shared something — a thought, a photo, a piece of work — and the replies came back sharp. A stranger called you stupid, ugly, worthless. Dozens of kind comments sit right beside it, yet your eyes keep returning to the one that cut. You reread it at night, compose clever comebacks, wonder whether they're right. Someone who doesn't know you spent thirty seconds typing, and now their words are living rent-free in your chest, hours or even days later.

## The Mindful Approach

The Buddha compared insults to a gift that is refused: if you don't accept it, it stays with the giver. Hate comments are often thrown by people in pain, projecting frustration onto a screen with no face attached. That doesn't make them harmless, but it does mean they are not an accurate mirror. Your task is not to win the exchange, but to decide what you allow to settle in your mind.

- **Decline the gift.** Notice the moment you start rehearsing the comment, and gently say to yourself, "This isn't mine to keep." You can read words without accepting them as truth. Refusing to internalize cruelty is not denial; it's a choice about which voices get to shape your self-image.
- **See the person behind the handle.** Behind most vicious remarks is someone bored, hurting, or seeking a reaction. Holding that in mind doesn't excuse them, but it softens the sting. A little compassion for a stranger's unhappiness can loosen hate's grip on you far faster than a retort ever could.
- **Weigh the kind voices equally.** The mind clings to negativity — one insult can outweigh fifty compliments. Deliberately rebalance the scale. Read the supportive replies slowly, let them land. They are just as real as the harsh one, and far more representative of the people who actually value what you do.

## A Practice for Today

When a hurtful comment surfaces in your mind, pause and take three slow breaths. With each exhale, silently repeat: "I do not accept this gift." Then, rather than replying, mute, block, or simply close the app. Afterwards, find one encouraging message you've received — from a friend or a follower — and read it twice, letting it sink in. You get to choose which words become part of your story. Let kindness have the louder voice.
