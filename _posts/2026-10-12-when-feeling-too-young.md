You walk into the meeting and sense them sizing you up. Your ideas get polite nods and then quiet dismissal, while the same point from someone older is praised as insightful. People call you "kid" or assume you don't understand. You feel pressure to act older, to prove you belong, to hide your enthusiasm lest it look naive. It's maddening to have something real to offer and watch it be discounted simply because of when you happened to be born.

## The Mindful Approach

Being underestimated because of age is a genuine frustration, and your ideas deserve a fair hearing. Yet Buddhist wisdom reminds us that insight isn't measured in years. Some of the Buddha's awakened disciples were young, and he valued clarity wherever it appeared. Your freshness is not a weakness to hide. Paired with humility and patience, it becomes a rare kind of strength that can open doors seniority alone never could.

- **Value your fresh perspective.** You see things others have stopped noticing, precisely because you haven't been conditioned by years of "how it's always done." That vantage point is useful. Instead of apologizing for being new, offer your observations with calm confidence. Questions that seem naive often reveal assumptions everyone else forgot to examine long ago.
- **Pair confidence with humility.** Those with more experience genuinely know things you don't yet know. Listening deeply, asking for guidance, and learning from mistakes won't diminish you. It builds credibility. The combination of openness and self-assurance is disarming; people tend to take seriously someone who is both eager to learn and willing to speak honestly.
- **Let consistency speak louder than age.** You won't win respect with a single argument. Trust grows through reliability: showing up prepared, following through, handling setbacks with grace. Over time, your track record becomes more persuasive than any assumption about your youth. Patience here isn't passivity; it's letting your work steadily reshape how others see you.

## A Practice for Today

Before your next interaction where you expect to be underestimated, take a few breaths and set an intention: "I'll offer what I see, and listen for what I don't know." Then share one observation clearly, without shrinking or overcompensating, and ask one sincere question of someone more experienced. Afterward, notice how it felt to hold both confidence and curiosity together. Your age is simply where you are on the path. Walk it with steadiness, and wisdom will meet you there.
