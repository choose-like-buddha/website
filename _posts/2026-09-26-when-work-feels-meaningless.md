You finish another spreadsheet, send another update, sit through another meeting about a meeting — and a hollow question echoes underneath it all: what is any of this for? The days blur. You're competent, maybe even praised, yet something inside feels flat and far away. You scroll through stories of people with callings and wonder if you missed yours. The paycheck arrives, the weekends come and go, and the sense of purpose you once expected from work keeps slipping further out of reach.

## The Mindful Approach

The feeling of meaninglessness is worth listening to — it may be pointing toward real change. But it can also come from expecting work alone to supply a sense of significance it was never built to give. Zen teachers speak of chopping wood and carrying water: the most ordinary labor becomes meaningful when done with full presence and care. Meaning is often less about what you do than about how, and for whom, you do it.

- **Trace the thread to a person.** Almost every task eventually touches a human being — a customer who gets an answer, a colleague whose day becomes easier, a family your income supports. Follow your work downstream until you find someone it helps. Purpose tends to hide not in grand missions but in these quiet connections.
- **Bring craft to the mundane.** Choose one routine task and do it with complete attention, as if it were an art. Notice the satisfaction of a clean email, a tidy file, a problem solved elegantly. Presence transforms tedium. When the mind stops rushing past the work, the work itself begins to feel alive.
- **Let work be one source, not the only one.** If your job is a means rather than a calling, that's allowed. Meaning can live in relationships, creativity, service, and practice outside office hours. Releasing the demand that work be everything often makes it feel lighter — and frees energy to explore what truly calls you.

## A Practice for Today

Pick one task you'd normally rush through with a sigh. Before starting, pause and ask, "Who does this ultimately serve?" Picture that person as specifically as you can. Then do the task slowly and carefully, giving it your whole attention for just ten minutes. Afterward, notice any shift in how it felt. Meaning is rarely discovered in the future — it is cultivated in the present, one attended moment at a time. Even small work can be done with a big heart.
