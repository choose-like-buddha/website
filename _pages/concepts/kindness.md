# Kindness

In Buddhist practice, kindness is *mettā*: goodwill, an open wish for someone's wellbeing. It's easy to confuse that with being pleasant. In a real moment the kind response might be the gentle one, or it might be the one that doesn't cushion the blow.

{% include concept-poles.html id="kindness" %}

## The Tension

You have to give feedback that will sting. A friend is upset and wants comfort, but part of what they're upset about is their own doing. Your child failed at something, and it matters that they feel it.

**Softening it** means cushioning the hard thing, choosing words and timing that make it easier to take in. **Letting it land** means not rushing to rescue someone from a feeling that might be doing useful work.

Softening can be compassion, or it can be your own discomfort with someone else's pain. Letting it land can be respect, or it can be coldness wearing respect's clothes.

## What the Tradition Says

The *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (Sn 1.8) asks for goodwill as wide as a mother's for her only child. That image includes more tenderness than people usually expect, and more firmness.

The *Abhaya Sutta* (MN 58) makes the same point from another side. If a small child puts a stick or a stone in its mouth, the Buddha says, you take it out at once, *even if it draws blood*, out of compassion for the child. Goodwill sometimes hurts.

Mettā describes what you wish for the other person. It doesn't tell you to make every moment comfortable.

## How It Shows Up in the Practice

Each day includes one Kindness item: a check-in on whether this came up, a question about how you handled it, or a situation where you choose how you would. Over time, [Patterns]({{ '/practice-map/' | relative_url }}) shows whether you tend to soften or let things land, and whether what you'd do matches what you did.

## A Practice for Today

When you next feel the urge to soften something, pause and ask: *Who is this softening for, them or me?* Then choose either way. The practice is knowing which one you picked.
