# Letting Go

Clinging (*upādāna*) sits near the centre of the Buddha's account of suffering, so "let it go" gets said a lot. Said too quickly, it turns into "don't care". Some things deserve to stay open: an unresolved wrong, a lesson you haven't finished learning, a loss that's still being grieved.

{% include concept-poles.html id="letting-go" %}

## The Tension

An old argument comes back to mind in the shower. A friend never apologised. A plan failed, and you keep going back over why.

**Setting it down** means choosing to stop carrying it, not because it didn't matter but because carrying it no longer helps. **Keeping it live** means deliberately leaving it open because it still has something to teach you, or something still needs to be done about it.

Setting it down can be freedom or it can be suppression. Keeping it live can be integrity or it can be a grudge.

## What the Tradition Says

In the *Alagaddūpama Sutta* (MN 22), the Buddha describes a man who builds a raft to cross a dangerous river. Once he's across, should he carry the raft on his head for the rest of the journey out of gratitude? No. The raft was for crossing, not for keeping.

The simile doesn't say the raft was worthless. It says there's a time to use something and a time to put it down, and the skill is telling which is which.

## How It Shows Up in the Practice

Each day includes one Letting Go item. It might ask whether something came up that you had to hold or release, what you actually did with it, or what you would do in a given situation. [Patterns]({{ '/practice-map/' | relative_url }}) shows whether you tend to set things down or keep them live, and where your intentions and your actions part ways.

## A Practice for Today

Name one thing you're still carrying. Ask: *Is this still a raft, or am I just carrying wood?* If it still has a use, keep it live on purpose and decide what it's for. If not, set it down, and expect to set it down again tomorrow.
