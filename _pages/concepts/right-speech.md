# Right Speech

Right Speech is one factor of the Noble Eightfold Path. It's usually summarised as a list of things to avoid: lying, divisive speech, harsh speech and idle chatter. Most people who practise already avoid the obvious versions. The hard part comes when every option is honest and you have to decide what to say.

{% include concept-poles.html id="right-speech" %}

## The Tension

A colleague's presentation has a flaw they haven't noticed. A friend asks whether you liked the meal they cooked. Your sister is about to make a decision you think is a mistake.

**Sparing them** means holding back something true because it isn't kind, isn't timely, or isn't yours to say. **Telling them** means saying the true thing because silence would let them walk into something they'd want to know about.

Neither one is a lie. Both can come from care, and both can come from fear: you might spare someone to avoid an awkward moment, or tell them to feel righteous.

## What the Tradition Says

In the *Abhaya Sutta* (MN 58), the Buddha gives a test for speech that fits this tension well. He speaks only what is **true** and **beneficial**, and he knows the **right time** to say it, *even when it is unwelcome*. True words that wouldn't help, he doesn't say. The sutta treats timing and benefit as part of the decision, not as afterthoughts.

The *Vācā Sutta* (AN 5.198) adds two more marks of well-spoken words: they are spoken **gently**, and with **a mind of goodwill**.

So the tradition doesn't settle "spare or tell" for you. It gives you the questions to ask in the moment.

## How It Shows Up in the Practice

One of each day's five items is about Right Speech. It might be a check-in on whether a moment like this came up, a question about what you actually said, or a situation where you choose what you would say. Over time, [Patterns]({{ '/practice-map/' | relative_url }}) shows where you lean between sparing and telling, and how far what you'd say is from what you said.

For many people this is where the gap is widest. We picture ourselves as the friend who speaks up, and then we don't.

## A Practice for Today

Before the next thing you say that matters, run the three checks: *Is it true? Is it beneficial? Is this the time?* If all three are yes and you still hold back, notice what you're protecting. If one is no and you still want to say it, notice that too.
